\begin{abstract}

\textbf{Purpose:} The international expansion of railway technology has increased demand for bilingual vocational education, yet railway regulations, technical terminology and teaching materials remain difficult to access consistently across Chinese and English. Because most vocational institutions and local research teams work with limited computing resources, this study develops and evaluates a railway-domain large language model that answers bilingual railway questions on a single workstation.

\textbf{Design/methodology/approach:} The study addresses the problem with two methods. The first is a hybrid evidence-fusion method: BM25 lexical retrieval and BGE-M3 dense retrieval are fused by reciprocal rank fusion, and the top-ranked railway evidence passages are inserted into the prompt as the knowledge context for answering. The second is a vertical-domain adaptation method: completion-only QLoRA specialises Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and GLM-4-9B-Chat-HF on reviewed railway question-answer pairs, with the base weights frozen in 4-bit NF4. Both methods were evaluated on a held-out bilingual railway set against the original models and an unadapted Qwen3-14B reference under an identical single-GPU boundary.

\textbf{Findings:} Evidence fusion produced the highest retrieval quality in both languages, reaching Evidence Recall@5 of 0.715 in Chinese and 0.708 in English, and fusing both language fields gave the best cross-language balance. Vertical fine-tuning raised held-out character-level F1 from 0.189 to 0.398 in Chinese and from 0.437 to 0.648 in English for Qwen2.5-7B. Combining both methods gave the best answer quality: Qwen QLoRA reached an answer F1 of 0.660 in Chinese and 0.651 in English under fused evidence, and adding that evidence improved answer F1 for every evaluated generator in both languages.

\textbf{Originality/value:} The study shows that lexical-dense evidence fusion and parameter-efficient vertical adaptation are complementary rather than substitutable mechanisms for building a domain model, especially in specialised vertical domains. Reporting the two methods under one resource boundary and one held-out protocol separates the gain obtained from retrieved railway knowledge from the gain obtained from domain-specific fine-tuning, and it gives a reproducible procedure for developing vertical-domain language models without multi-GPU training.

\textbf{Keywords:} railway domain large language model; bilingual vocational education; hybrid retrieval; reciprocal rank fusion; retrieval-augmented generation; QLoRA

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The international expansion of high-speed railway construction, operation and technical cooperation has increased demand for bilingual vocational education \citep{lawrence2019hsr,xu2018education}. Railway personnel and learners working across national and linguistic settings must understand operating rules, safety procedures, equipment terminology and teaching materials that are frequently written first in Chinese. The practical difficulty is therefore not limited to translating isolated terms. Educational support must connect a question posed in either language to the correct railway knowledge and then produce an answer that stays inside the boundaries of that knowledge.

General-purpose large language models can organise such knowledge fluently, but they are not trained on the terminology, evidence boundaries and procedural conventions of a specific railway corpus. Railway-oriented studies have built specialised models for safety, track management, prognostics, driver advice and interactive training \citep{zheng2023trafficsafety,li2024rschat,song2025railway,wang2024phm,luo2025driver,li2025training,huang2026emergency}, and broader reviews continue to identify reliability and deployment as open problems for transport applications \citep{wandelt2024transport}. Two weaknesses recur across that literature. First, the models are usually developed for one task and one language, so it is unclear whether the same approach transfers to a bilingual vocational setting where the same knowledge item must be answered in Chinese and in English. Second, the contribution of retrieved external knowledge is rarely separated from the contribution of model adaptation, which makes it difficult to tell which of the two mechanisms actually produced an observed improvement.

Computing availability is a second and equally binding constraint. Many vocational institutions and local research teams cannot maintain multi-GPU training clusters or rely continuously on commercial cloud inference. Full-parameter adaptation of large models is therefore difficult to reproduce and expensive to sustain in exactly the institutions that need a railway-domain assistant most. Quantised low-rank adaptation offers a realistic alternative: a 7B or 9B model can be specialised on a single 24 GB GPU while the base weights remain frozen. The resource constraint is consequently part of the research problem, not a secondary deployment detail.

This study addresses the problem with two methods.

The first method targets knowledge access and is referred to below as \emph{evidence fusion}. BM25 lexical ranking and BGE-M3 dense ranking are computed in parallel and fused by reciprocal rank fusion, and the highest-ranked railway evidence passages are inserted into the prompt before generation. This method is what allows a question in either language to reach the correct reviewed railway record and to be answered from that record rather than from parametric memory.

The second method targets domain behaviour and is referred to below as \emph{vertical adaptation}. Completion-only QLoRA trains low-rank adapters on reviewed railway question-answer pairs while the 4-bit quantised base model stays frozen, so the model learns railway answer vocabulary and answer structure without full-parameter training.

The two methods are then evaluated. Retrieval is scored by evidence-level metrics; adaptation is scored as a before-and-after comparison on held-out railway questions; the combined system is scored across five generators under identical cached evidence; and the resource cost of every condition is measured on the same workstation. This design answers a question that the existing railway-domain literature leaves open: whether improved knowledge access and improved domain behaviour are substitutes or complements, and what each costs inside a fixed hardware budget.

The study makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item It applies hybrid lexical-dense evidence fusion to bilingual railway vocational question answering and shows that fused retrieval gives the best evidence recall in both languages and that a bilingual field composition gives the best cross-language balance.
\item It applies completion-only QLoRA vertical adaptation to two model families and quantifies the held-out bilingual gain with paired significance testing.
\item It constructs a high-quality Chinese-English parallel railway corpus and reports a controlled joint evaluation on the same held-out records showing that the two mechanisms are complementary but partially overlapping, and that vertical adaptation contributes the larger share of the gain on the same base model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

This section reviews the three lines of work that the present study builds on, states what each line actually contributed, and identifies the gap that remains.

\subsection{Railway and transportation domain language models}

A first line of work adapts general language models to railway and transportation tasks. Zheng \emph{et al.} (2023) constructed a transportation-safety instruction corpus and fine-tuned a pre-trained model into a domain expert, demonstrating that domain instruction tuning changes task behaviour rather than only surface style. Li \emph{et al.} (2024) built RSChat, an intelligent question-answering model over railway safety knowledge, which is the closest published antecedent to a railway vocational question-answering system. Song \emph{et al.} (2025) specialised a language model for railway track management and reported domain-specific gains over a general model on track-management questions. Luo \emph{et al.} (2025) developed a driver advisory system for high-speed trains in which the language model produces operational advice under timetable and signalling constraints. Li \emph{et al.} (2025) evaluated a human-computer interactive training assistant for rail systems on dispatching and professional-training scenarios, which is the one prior study in this group that explicitly addresses railway education.

Two further strands extend the same idea. Huang \emph{et al.} (2026) generated emergency operation schemes for urban rail transit train door systems with a retrieval-augmented model, showing that external evidence is needed when the required procedure is not reliably memorised. Wang and Li (2024) surveyed the potential applications, limitations and required solutions for language models in railway prognostics and health management, and Lahnalampi (2024) examined their use in rail design projects. At a broader level, Wandelt \emph{et al.} (2024) reviewed language models for intelligent transportation and concluded that reliability, domain grounding and deployment cost remain unresolved.

What this line establishes is that railway tasks benefit from domain-specific language modelling, and that retrieval becomes necessary when the answer depends on a specific regulation or procedure. What it does not establish is a bilingual setting: the reviewed systems are built and evaluated in a single language, so the cross-language retrieval question, that is, whether the same evidence can serve a Chinese question and an English question equally well, is left open.

\subsection{Retrieval-augmented generation and hybrid evidence fusion}

A second line of work supplies the knowledge-access mechanism. Lewis \emph{et al.} (2020) introduced retrieval-augmented generation, in which a parametric generator is conditioned on documents retrieved from an external non-parametric index. This organisation is directly relevant to railway vocational education because regulations, terminology records and teaching materials are revised over time, and an external index can be updated without retraining the generator.

Within that framework, the retrieval component has been developed along two complementary directions. Robertson and Zaragoza (2009) formalised the probabilistic relevance framework and its BM25 instantiation, which remains strong when a query and a document share exact terms such as equipment names, regulation identifiers and standard codes. Chen \emph{et al.} (2024) introduced BGE-M3, a multilingual embedding model that produces dense representations for semantically related but lexically different text, which matters when a Chinese and an English question express the same need with different wording. Because the two retrievers score on incommensurable scales, Cormack \emph{et al.} (2009) proposed reciprocal rank fusion, which merges ranked lists using only rank positions and therefore requires no score normalisation. H\"ubscher \emph{et al.} (2021) addressed how knowledge and process descriptions should be represented and presented in dynamic, communication-intensive environments, which is relevant to how a domain record should be structured so that it stays usable by both a human reviewer and a downstream model. In the legal-document setting, Yang \emph{et al.} (2024) surveyed question answering over long professional documents and showed that retrieval strategy and answer verification are the two decisive design choices in that genre. Chang \emph{et al.} (2024) surveyed language-model evaluation and argued that distinct capabilities require distinct metrics instead of a single aggregate score.

This line therefore supplies the first method used here: lexical and dense retrieval fused at rank level. Its evaluations, however, are predominantly monolingual and mostly assume a single-language index. The question of how the index should be composed when every knowledge item exists in two languages, and whether a bilingual field composition beats a language-specific one, is not settled by this literature.

\subsection{Parameter-efficient vertical-domain adaptation}

A third line of work supplies the adaptation mechanism. Dettmers \emph{et al.} (2023) introduced QLoRA, which back-propagates through a frozen 4-bit NF4 quantised base model into low-rank adapters, reducing training memory enough to fine-tune multi-billion-parameter models on a single device. Ding \emph{et al.} (2023) and Wang \emph{et al.} (2025) surveyed parameter-efficient fine-tuning and catalogued the trade-offs between adapter capacity, trainable parameter count and task performance. Lv \emph{et al.} (2024) examined full-parameter fine-tuning under limited resources and documented how sensitive such training is to quantisation, sequence length and optimiser state, which is why the present study fixes those settings explicitly rather than leaving them to defaults.

A consistent warning runs through this literature: adaptation is not automatically beneficial. Tian \emph{et al.} (2024) showed that fine-tuning can improve factuality, but Barnett \emph{et al.} (2024) demonstrated that fine-tuning frequently underperforms simpler alternatives when the evaluation protocol is not controlled. In the educational setting that motivates this study, Kasneci \emph{et al.} (2023) and UNESCO (2023) identified factual reliability, learner over-reliance and opaque provenance as the main risks of generative models in teaching. Schwartz \emph{et al.} (2020) argued more generally that predictive quality should be reported together with its computational cost, and that reporting quality alone misrepresents the value of an efficient method. Adapter size by itself therefore does not establish low inference cost, and a before-and-after design plus measured resource use is required before an adaptation gain can be claimed.

\subsection{Research gap}

The three lines above supply a domain motivation, a knowledge-access mechanism and an adaptation mechanism, but they have not been combined under one controlled protocol. Railway-domain studies are essentially monolingual and usually report a single mechanism. Hybrid-retrieval studies are essentially monolingual in their index design and rarely measure resource cost. Parameter-efficient adaptation studies rarely test whether the observed gain survives once external evidence is also supplied.

The present study fills that gap by applying both methods to the same bilingual railway corpus and evaluating them on the same held-out records under one hardware boundary. This allows three questions to be answered together: whether fused bilingual retrieval improves evidence access over either retriever alone, whether completion-only QLoRA improves held-out railway question answering, and whether the two gains are additive or overlapping when the adapted models also receive fused evidence.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem definition and resource boundary}

The task is bilingual railway question answering over reviewed regulations, terminology records and vocational teaching material. Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the governed knowledge base of railway records and let $q$ be a question written in Chinese or English. The system must return an answer $\hat{y}$ in the language of $q$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}=f_{\theta_0,\theta_A}\big(q,\;\mathcal{R}(q,\mathcal{D})\big),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}(q,\mathcal{D})$ is the evidence returned by the retrieval function and $f$ is a generator whose base weights $\theta_0$ are frozen and whose adapter weights $\theta_A$ carry the domain adaptation. Equation (1) states the two methods of this study directly: $\mathcal{R}$ is the evidence-fusion method, and $\theta_A$ is the vertical-adaptation method. When $\mathcal{R}(q,\mathcal{D})=\varnothing$ the generator answers from parametric memory alone, which is the no-retrieval condition used as a baseline throughout.

The resource boundary is one NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of memory and 32 GB of system RAM, with no multi-GPU training and no required cloud inference. Writing $M$ for the peak GPU memory of a condition, every configuration must satisfy

\begin{equation}
M \le 24\ \text{GB},
\end{equation}

and the reported scores are only comparable because Equation (2) holds for all of them. This constraint is what makes NF4 quantisation and low-rank adaptation necessary rather than optional, since full-parameter training of the selected 7B and 9B models exceeds the boundary.

\subsection{Overall framework}

Figure 1 places the two methods on the same system. Evidence fusion changes what the generator is allowed to see, whereas vertical adaptation changes how the generator answers. Because the two mechanisms act at different points in the architecture, their contributions can be distinguished by holding one of them fixed and varying the other.

\subsection{Method one: bilingual hybrid retrieval with evidence fusion}

The first method solves the knowledge-access problem. Railway questions mix exact identifiers with paraphrased technical descriptions, and no single retriever handles both. The method therefore computes two rankings in parallel and merges them at rank level.

(2) Shared neural encoding: one frozen BGE-M3 encoder maps the query and the candidate evidence into the same 1024-dimensional space. (4) Evidence-conditioned generation with vertical adaptation: the serialised context $[\text{Instr}]\,|\,[\text{Q}]\,|\,\text{evidence}\,|\,[\text{A}]$ is decoded by a repeated causal decoder whose frozen weights $W_0$ receive a low-rank update $dW=BA$ (rank 64, alpha 16). The evaluation block outside the model carries no gradient; it reports the question-answering scores used as the primary results here, and marks the downstream uses the system supports once deployed, namely citing a generated answer against its evidence, translating an answer between Chinese and English, checking whether its claims are supported by the retrieved passages, and measuring latency and GPU memory. The lexical ranking scores a candidate passage $d$ against query $q$ with BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25}:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\operatorname{IDF}(t)\cdot
\frac{f(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{f(t,d)+k_1\left(1-b+b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)}
\cdot\bigl(1+0.05\min\!\left(f(t,q),3\right)\bigr),
\end{equation}

where $f(t,d)$ is the frequency of term $t$ in $d$, $f(t,q)$ is its frequency in the query, $|d|$ is the passage length, $\mathrm{avgdl}$ is the average passage length in the index, and $k_1$ and $b$ control term-frequency saturation and length normalisation. The implementation sets $k_1=1.5$ and $b=0.75$, and the final factor mildly up-weights a query term that occurs more than once, saturating at three occurrences. The corpus is small enough that the index is held in memory, so no external search library is involved. Then

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{IDF}(t)=\ln\!\left(\frac{N-n(t)+0.5}{n(t)+0.5}+1\right)
\end{equation}

with $N$ the number of indexed passages and $n(t)$ the number containing $t$. Equation (3) rewards exact overlap, which is what preserves equipment names, clause numbers and standard codes that a dense encoder may blur.

The dense ranking embeds the query and the passage with BGE-M3 \citep{chen2024bgem3} and scores them by cosine similarity:

\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{dense}}(q,d)=\frac{\mathbf{e}_q^{\top}\mathbf{e}_d}{\lVert\mathbf{e}_q\rVert_2\,\lVert\mathbf{e}_d\rVert_2},
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{e}_q,\mathbf{e}_d\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}$. Equation (5) rewards semantic equivalence, which is what recovers an English passage for a Chinese query that shares no surface term with it.

Because Equations (3) and (5) are not on a common scale, the two rankings are merged by reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009rrf}:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{RRF}(d)=\sum_{r\in\{\mathrm{BM25},\,\mathrm{dense}\}}\frac{1}{k_0+\rank_r(d)},
\end{equation}

where $\rank_r(d)$ is the position of $d$ in ranking $r$ and $k_0$ is a smoothing constant. A passage absent from a retriever's candidate list contributes zero for that retriever. Equation (6) depends only on rank position, so no score calibration between the lexical and dense retrievers is needed. The candidate pool is fixed at 50 per retriever and $k_0=60$, and the fusion is applied before the final cut-off is chosen.

The fused list is truncated to the top-$k$ passages, and these are serialised into the prompt as the knowledge context:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}(q,\mathcal{D})=\underset{d\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adm}}}{\operatorname{top}\text{-}k}\ \operatorname{RRF}(d),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adm}}$ is the admissible subset of the knowledge base, and the prompt is assembled as

\begin{equation}
x=\big[\,\text{Instr}\,\big]\;\big|\;\big[\,q\,\big]\;\big|\;\big[E_1\big]\ldots\big[E_k\big]\;\big|\;\big[\,\text{A}\,\big],
\end{equation}

where each $E_i$ is rendered into the prompt as its numbered label, its source document and the passage text, while the retrieval object also carries the record identifier, source document and task type that the evaluation needs. Equation (8) is the point at which the first method hands control to the generator: the tags $[E_i]$ give the model an addressable evidence set, and the experiment varies $k$ from one to eight to measure how much evidence is worth supplying.

Two design choices distinguish this implementation. First, the same encoder is used for the query and for the evidence, so no cross-lingual alignment step is required at query time. Second, held-out records are removed before ranking: the lexical index is built with test-split and reserved RAG-test records excluded, and the dense query carries the same exclusion in its database predicate. This guarantees that a held-out record cannot be returned as evidence for its own question. The approved-only condition is a separate control that is enforced while candidates are returned rather than assumed of the corpus, and the two controls are reported separately in the results.

\subsection{Method two: completion-only QLoRA vertical adaptation}

The second method solves the domain-behaviour problem. A general model produces fluent answers that use the wrong register, omit the procedural detail expected in a railway answer, or answer a regulation question by paraphrase instead of by clause content. The method corrects this by training low-rank adapters on reviewed railway question-answer pairs while the base model stays frozen.

For a pre-trained weight matrix $W_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, the adapted forward pass is

\begin{equation}
h=W_0x+\Delta Wx=W_0x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx,
\end{equation}

where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ are the trainable low-rank factors, $r$ is the rank and $\alpha$ is a fixed scaling constant \citep{dettmers2023qlora}. Only $A$ and $B$ receive gradients, so the trainable parameter count is $r(d+k)$ rather than $dk$; with $r=64$ this is a small fraction of the full matrix, which is what makes the method fit inside Equation (2).

The frozen base weights are stored in 4-bit NF4 format. A block of weights is dequantised with per-block scale and zero-point constants before the forward pass:

\begin{equation}
W_0 \approx s_b\left(q_b-c_b\right),
\end{equation}

where $q_b$ is the quantised block, and $s_b$ and $c_b$ are the block scale and offset. Equation (10) is a lossy representation of the base weights, and the adapter in Equation (9) is what restores task capacity at that reduced precision.

Supervision is applied to answer tokens only. With $m_t=1$ for an answer token and $m_t=0$ for a prompt token, the training objective is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta_A)=-\frac{1}{\sum_t m_t}\sum_t m_t\log p_{\theta_0,\theta_A}\!\left(y_t\mid y_{<t},x\right),
\end{equation}

where $\theta_0$ denotes the frozen NF4 base weights and $\theta_A=\{\theta_A^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}$ the trainable adapters in their respective layers. Equation (11) is the reason the method is called completion-only: masking the prompt positions prevents the model from spending capacity on reproducing instructions and concentrates it on the railway answer, and it keeps the training signal identical to the inference-time task defined in Equation (1).

The adaptation corpus is built only from approved records and is split by knowledge-pair identifier, task type and source document. Chinese and English variants of one knowledge item always receive the same partition assignment, so a bilingual pair cannot straddle the training and held-out boundary, and no knowledge-pair identifier is shared across partitions. Completion-only masking labels every prompt token with $-100$; the loss is computed only on answer tokens.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Retrieval is scored at the evidence level rather than the record level, because a different admissible record may legitimately contain the evidence required to answer the question. Evidence-equivalent Recall@$k$ is one when at least one of the first $k$ candidates satisfies the matching rule:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Recall@}k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists\, d\in \operatorname{top}\text{-}k(q):\ \operatorname{match}(d,q)\right],
\end{equation}

where $\operatorname{match}(d,q)$ holds when the candidate shares the record identifier with the gold evidence, when the whitespace-normalised gold and candidate evidence contain one another in either direction, or when the candidate comes from the same source document and contains the gold answer. Mean reciprocal rank is computed over the rank of the first matching candidate:

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MRR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{\rank_q^{*}},
\end{equation}

where $\rank_q^{*}$ is the rank of the first matching candidate for $q$, and contributes zero when no candidate matches.

Generation is scored by character-level overlap between the prediction $\hat{y}$ and the reference $y$, computed on multisets of characters after answer normalisation and whitespace removal:

\begin{equation}
P=\frac{|\hat{y}\cap y|}{|\hat{y}|},\qquad
R=\frac{|\hat{y}\cap y|}{|y|},\qquad
F1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\end{equation}

In the retrieval-augmented experiments the same overlap is computed over individual Chinese characters and lower-cased Latin and alphanumeric tokens. The two definitions are reported within their own settings and are not treated as interchangeable absolute scores. Reference containment is reported alongside $F1$ as a stricter indicator of whether the normalised reference occurs in the prediction.

All generator comparisons use paired samples. Differences between conditions are reported with two-sided paired Wilcoxon tests, the paired effect size

\begin{equation}
d_z=\frac{\bar{d}}{s_d},
\end{equation}

where $\bar{d}$ is the mean of the per-sample differences and $s_d$ their standard deviation, and Holm correction across the pre-specified comparison family. Mean metrics are accompanied by 2,000-resample bootstrap 95 per cent confidence intervals.

Resource use is measured over 30 Chinese short questions, 30 English short questions and 30 regulation-oriented long-context questions, with one warm-up prompt at the start of each condition and three measured repetitions, giving 270 measurements per condition. Reported quantities are peak and static reserved GPU memory, first-token latency, mean end-to-end latency and generation throughput.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Corpus and experimental sample}

The governed knowledge base contains 37,661 approved records and 37,109 complete bilingual records, in which a Chinese and an English field share one knowledge-pair identifier. The sources are railway terminology, operating and safety regulations, and vocational textbook or concept material. From the approved bilingual pairs, 7,614 were selected under a task cap and split 80/10/10 by knowledge-pair identifier into 12,178 training, 1,524 validation and 1,526 held-out records, balanced equally between the two languages. Pair overlap is zero across all three partitions. The held-out partition contains 763 knowledge pairs across four source documents; three quarters of the terminology pairs reach the per-task cap, so the frozen sample is dominated by terminology, regulation and textbook tasks in that proportion.

Two evaluation samples are drawn from the held-out partition. The standalone bilingual question-answering sample uses all 763 pairs in both languages. The cross-source retrieval-augmented sample uses 400 pairs and 800 language-specific queries, covering 150 regulation, 127 terminology and 123 textbook or concept pairs across 17 task types. The same 400 pairs and the same cached evidence are used for every generator, so generator differences cannot arise from differences in retrieved context.

First, the two retrievers are not interchangeable and their weaknesses are language-dependent. BM25 is the strongest single retriever for English, reaching an Evidence Recall@1 of 0.570 and an MRR of 0.620, which reflects the many English queries that share exact terminology with their evidence. Dense retrieval is the stronger single retriever for Chinese at recall levels above one, reaching 0.690 Evidence Recall@5 against 0.620 for BM25, which reflects Chinese queries that are paraphrases rather than term matches. Neither retriever dominates, which is the precondition for fusion to be useful.

Second, fusion improves on both. The fused ranking attains the highest Evidence Recall@5 in Chinese (0.715) and in English (0.708) and the highest MRR in both languages (0.628 and 0.620). The gain is largest where the single retrievers disagree most: in Chinese, fusion raises Recall@1 from 0.520 to 0.570 while the dense retriever alone was 0.518, meaning the lexical ranking still contributed candidates that the dense ranking had missed.

Third, the improvement is not free. Mean retrieval latency rises from 69.5 ms for BM25 and 134.4 ms for dense retrieval to 217.6 ms for the fused Chinese condition, so fusion is a quality-latency trade-off rather than a uniform efficiency gain. Filtering to approved records changes nothing in this frozen experiment, because the governed corpus holds 37{,}661 approved records out of 37{,}664, so the admissible index and the approved index are the same index in practice; the filter matters for correctness of the protocol rather than for the measured scores.

Figure 2 shows how the final evidence cut-off interacts with quality and latency. Recall rises monotonically with $k$, from 0.570 at $k=1$ to 0.743 in Chinese and 0.723 in English at $k=8$; the largest single step is between $k=1$ and $k=3$, after which the curve flattens. Retrieval latency is almost flat across the same range, staying near 218 ms in Chinese and 209 ms in English, because each setting reuses the same 50-candidate pool from each retriever and only the truncation point changes. The evidence supplied to the generator can therefore be increased at essentially no additional retrieval cost, although a longer context still consumes generation time and memory.

\subsection{Bilingual index composition}

Because every knowledge item exists in both languages, the index can be built from the Chinese field, from the English field, from the source evidence field, or from both language fields. The four compositions were compared on the same sample, and the result argues for the bilingual index.

Under fused retrieval, the bilingual index reaches the highest balanced mean across languages, with an Evidence Recall@5 of 0.718 in Chinese and 0.675 in English, giving a mean of 0.696. The English-field index is better on English alone (0.713) but much weaker in Chinese (0.560), and the Chinese-field index is better in Chinese (0.653) but weaker in English (0.530). The source-evidence index is the weakest overall (0.638 Chinese, 0.448 English). A language-specific index therefore wins on its own language and loses on the other, which is the wrong trade-off for a bilingual vocational assistant, where a learner may ask in either language about the same knowledge item. Indexing both language fields is what keeps the two languages within a comparable range without a per-language index switch.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why evidence fusion helps, and where it stops helping}

The retrieval results show that the two retrievers solve different parts of the bilingual access problem. BM25 is strongest for English, where queries frequently reuse the exact terminology of the evidence, while dense retrieval is strongest for Chinese, where paraphrased questions do not share surface terms with the source passage. Fusing them at rank level recovers candidates from both and produces the best evidence recall and MRR in both languages, which is the behaviour the method was chosen for: reciprocal rank fusion needs no score calibration between a lexical model and a vector model, so it can be applied to whatever pair of retrievers a domain happens to offer.

The bilingual index result is the more specific finding for vocational education. A language-specific index wins on its own language and loses badly on the other, while indexing both language fields keeps Chinese and English within a comparable range. For an institution that serves learners who ask in either language about the same knowledge item, that balance is worth more than a peak score in one language.

The limits of the method are equally clear. Fusion costs roughly three times the latency of BM25, and increasing the evidence cut-off eventually lengthens the prompt, which shifts the cost from retrieval to generation rather than removing it. The answer-quality results add a further caution: higher evidence recall did not translate into higher answer overlap in every condition, since BM25 outperformed fused retrieval for two generators. Retrieval metrics are therefore necessary but not sufficient for choosing a strategy, and the choice should be validated against the generator that will actually be deployed.

\subsection{Why vertical adaptation helps, and what it does not replace}

Completion-only QLoRA raises held-out railway question answering substantially in both languages for both model families. Two features of the result matter for practice. First, the gains concentrate where the original model was weakest: the Chinese scores roughly double for both models, while the English score of the already stronger GLM-4-9B barely moves. This is what would be expected if the method supplies missing domain vocabulary and answer structure rather than uniformly improving generation. Second, the adapted 7B model outperforms an unadapted 14B model by a wide margin in both languages, which is the practical argument for vertical adaptation under a hardware constraint: at this scale, domain-specific adaptation buys more than a larger general model.

The joint evaluation is the part that distinguishes this study from single-mechanism reports. The results show that the two methods are complementary but partially overlapping. Evidence fusion improves every generator, yet the improvement shrinks as adaptation grows, from 0.141 for the original Qwen2.5-7B to 0.030 for the adapted one in Chinese. The overlap is expected, since both mechanisms supply railway knowledge by different routes, one at query time and one in the parameters, and it does not make either method redundant. Comparing the two mechanisms on equal footing for the same base model shows that vertical adaptation contributes the larger share: Qwen2.5-7B gains 0.368 in Chinese and 0.268 in English from adaptation alone, against 0.141 and 0.133 from fused evidence alone, and the same ordering holds for GLM-4-9B at 0.168 and 0.062 against 0.067 and 0.048. Adaptation and retrieval are nevertheless not interchangeable, because the best result in every language condition is obtained when the adapted model also receives fused evidence. The practical conclusion is that a vertical-domain deployment should invest in both, while expecting the marginal return of evidence fusion to fall as adaptation improves.

The same evaluation also exposes a genuine cost. The adapted models use roughly three times the reserved GPU memory of their originals and generate at roughly half the throughput. Adaptation is affordable inside a single-workstation boundary but is not free, and any deployment decision should weigh the measured quality gain in Table IV against the measured cost in Table VI.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three limitations bound these conclusions. The study uses one frozen corpus split, one epoch and one principal random seed, so the paired significance tests describe sample-level differences between conditions and do not estimate variation across repeated training runs. The retrieval evaluation depends on a predefined evidence-equivalence rule, so reported recall reflects that rule rather than an exhaustive relevance judgement. And the resource measurements come from a single GPU model, which means the absolute memory and latency values should not be transferred to other hardware without re-measurement, even though the qualitative ordering is likely to hold. The comparison between the PyTorch conditions and the Ollama-served reference model is descriptive for the same reason.

\section{Conclusion}

This study developed a railway-domain language model for bilingual vocational education under a single-workstation resource constraint, and it addressed the problem with two methods. The first is hybrid evidence fusion, which combines BM25 lexical ranking and BGE-M3 dense ranking through reciprocal rank fusion and supplies the top-ranked railway evidence as the answering context; it achieved the best evidence recall and MRR in both languages, at 0.715 Evidence Recall@5 in Chinese and 0.708 in English, and a bilingual index composition gave the best cross-language balance. The second is completion-only QLoRA vertical adaptation, which specialises a frozen 4-bit base model on reviewed railway question-answer pairs; it raised held-out character-level F1 from 0.189 to 0.398 in Chinese and from 0.437 to 0.648 in English for Qwen2.5-7B, with all paired gains significant after Holm correction. The joint evaluation showed that the two methods are complementary but partially overlapping: fused evidence improved answer F1 for all five generators in both languages, yet its marginal gain fell from 0.141 to 0.030 in Chinese once the model had been adapted, while the adapted 7B model outperformed an unadapted 14B reference by a wide margin. Every configuration ran inside the 24 GB boundary, although adaptation roughly tripled reserved memory and halved throughput. For institutions that cannot maintain multi-GPU training, these results indicate that lexical-dense evidence fusion and parameter-efficient vertical adaptation should be adopted together, and that the quality gain of each should be reported alongside its measured resource cost.
